The Medicine Market

A drug starts as one of ten thousand molecules in a screening library and ends as something a pharmacist hands across a counter. In between are eight distinct businesses, and almost none of them are the company whose name is on the box. This is who does what, what each part is worth, and how much of it depends on a single supplier.

01

The chain, end to end

Nine businesses stacked bottom to top: what goes into a drug, who tests it, who makes it, who owns it. A biotech with a promising molecule owns almost none of the machinery that turns it into a medicine — it rents the animals, the factory, the trial and the trucks.

The split between biotech and big pharma is a judgement, not a legal category: biotech here means a company still valued mainly on its pipeline, big pharma one selling a broad approved portfolio. Vertex and argenx sit close to the line.

02

What each layer is worth

Value is distributed nothing like the work. Everyone who supplies, tests, manufactures and ships for the industry — every sequencer, every factory, every trial — adds up to less than a fifth of it. The rest sits with the companies whose name goes on the box.

Boxes are market value, scaled by how much of each company is actually a medicines business — Johnson & Johnson counts at 65% because MedTech is not a drug business, Bayer at 45% because crop science is not either. Height is position in the stack: inputs at the bottom, the companies that own the drug at the top.

Readable areas compresses every box toward the middle so the smallest companies stay legible — the largest company here is some twenty thousand times the smallest, which at true scale is a box two pixels wide. Order is preserved and the number printed in each box is always the real one. True areas shows the honest proportions.

03

The ten-year gauntlet

Almost everything fails. Of a hundred molecules that make it as far as a first human trial — already the survivors of years of screening — about seven are ever approved. Phase 2 is where they die: it is the first test of whether the drug does anything at all, and roughly seven in ten do not.

04

Inside a trial

Each phase asks a different question, of a different number of people. Phase 1 asks whether the drug hurts anyone, and needs fifty. Phase 3 asks whether it beats the treatment patients already get, and needs a few thousand, across dozens of hospitals, for years. Every dot below is one participant, drawn at the same size throughout.

Middles, not rules. A trial's size and cost depend far more on the disease than on the phase: a Phase 3 in dermatology runs about $11.5M, one in pain and anaesthesia nearly $53M. Cost per participant is worked out here from the two figures beside it, not taken from a source.

05

Where the supply is thin

The expensive end of this industry is the visible one. The fragile end is a handful of chemical plants making ingredients nobody has heard of. Amoxicillin, ibuprofen and paracetamol are cheap, generic and ordinary — and their starting materials come, almost entirely, from one country.

Each bar measures a different thing — some are shares of world output, some are shares of what the United States imports — so the basis is written under every label. These are published estimates and should be read as ranges of a few points, not exact figures.

06

Made everywhere, invented somewhere

Manufacturing is spread almost evenly across four regions. Invention is not, and it is moving fast: a decade ago roughly half the world's early-stage drug programmes started in the United States and one in twelve in China. They are now close to level.

07

What one medicine costs

A month of metformin and one dose of Lenmeldy are both prescription medicines. One costs four dollars and is taken by a large fraction of everyone with type 2 diabetes; the other costs more than four million and is given to a few dozen children a year. The bar is drawn on a logarithmic scale, because nothing else fits.

US list prices. Almost nobody pays list — insurers negotiate rebates and patients pay a copay — but it is the only figure published for every drug, so it is the only one that compares across all of them.
